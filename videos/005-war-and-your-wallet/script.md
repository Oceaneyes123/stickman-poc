# How a War Thousands of Miles Away Reaches Your Wallet

**The Money Trap · Documentary narration draft · Approximately 10 minutes**

Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022 is the case study. The pump, grocery and borrowing examples are U.S.-based. Only the marked narration is spoken. The [claim ledger](sources.md) records the evidence, assumptions and beat sheet.

## 1. The same trip costs more

**Visual.** Open on an illustrated pump mid-fill. Match-cut between the first week of January and the mid-June weekly high. Label both as U.S. national averages and credit EIA. Keep the same amount of fuel in the illustration. (F01, A01)

<!-- NARRATION -->

On your way home from work, you stop for gas. The pump hits the amount you usually spend and keeps going. The tank still isn't full.

At the start of twenty twenty-two, regular gasoline in the United States averaged three dollars and twenty-eight cents a gallon. By the middle of June, the national average hit five dollars and one cent. If you still had to drive to work five days a week, that trip was taking more out of the same paycheck.

In between, on February twenty-fourth, Russia launched its full-scale invasion of Ukraine. For people in Ukraine, the immediate stakes were their lives and homes. Thousands of miles away, the invasion was driving up the cost of filling a tank. How did that happen when the United States bought so little of its imported crude oil from Russia?

<!-- /NARRATION -->

## 2. The oil your refinery never bought

**Visual.** Use a schematic cargo offer and two buyers to show competition for replacement supply. Follow a barrel through a refinery to the pump. Show refinery output and gasoline demand as separate contributors near the June marker. (F01–F03, A01)

<!-- NARRATION -->

The year before the full-scale invasion, Russia supplied about three percent of U.S. crude oil imports. But Russia was one of the world's big oil exporters. It mattered what happened to the oil that everyone else was buying, too.

Picture a refinery that ordinarily buys Russian crude. After the invasion, some buyers backed away from Russian oil. Some were affected by sanctions, others chose to stop buying it. If that refinery still needs to keep running, it has to find another seller. Now picture a second refinery that already buys from that seller. It suddenly has more competition for the next cargo. The second refinery can pay more even though it never bought a Russian barrel.

And that bidding can start before a shipment actually goes missing. If you're responsible for keeping a refinery running next month, waiting to see what happens is a risk of its own. The International Energy Agency saw this play out just after the invasion. Oil prices jumped before the agency had recorded a loss of supply.

Crude oil is the biggest single cost in a gallon of gasoline. When crude gets more expensive, a station can end up paying more for its next delivery. Gasoline demand had already picked up earlier in the year. By spring, U.S. refineries were running below their pre-pandemic rates and gasoline inventories were low. That left less fuel on hand to meet demand, which also helped push up pump prices.

<!-- /NARRATION -->

## 3. A harvest that can’t reach its buyers

**Visual.** Use an original map and storage illustration. Keep grain visible as access to Black Sea ports closes, then show the smaller rail and Danube alternatives. Use a freight invoice to connect diesel with delivery costs. (F03–F04, A01)

<!-- NARRATION -->

The fuel bill reaches further than the driver's seat. A truck hauling flour to a bakery runs on fuel, too. When diesel costs more, a carrier may charge more to make that same trip. By the time you see a loaf on a shelf, somebody has already paid to move the ingredients and to deliver the finished bread.

In Ukraine, an even bigger problem was getting grain out in the first place. Before the invasion, Russia and Ukraine together accounted for roughly thirty percent of the wheat sold abroad. When Russia blocked Ukraine's Black Sea ports, grain could still be sitting in storage while a buyer overseas scrambled to fill an order. Some exports moved by rail and through ports on the Danube River. Those alternatives helped, but they could only replace part of what had been going through the main ports.

A mill that has to keep making flour can look for wheat somewhere else. So can all the other mills facing the same problem. They can end up bidding against one another for wheat that was already going to be sold. That's how a port closure can affect the price of grain grown in a completely different country. In March, international wheat prices jumped nearly twenty percent in a month. Concerns about the American crop were adding pressure at the same time.

<!-- /NARRATION -->

## 4. Wheat down, bread up

**Visual.** Show July’s export agreement and the August 1 departure from Odesa. Then show July world wheat and U.S. bread moving in opposite directions, with independent labels and source credits. Reveal the stages between wheat and bread one at a time. (F05–F06, A01)

<!-- NARRATION -->

On August first, a ship called the Razoni left the Ukrainian port of Odesa carrying corn. It was the first commercial grain shipment under a deal signed in July to let food sail out of three Ukrainian ports again. The price of wheat on world markets had already fallen in July. The United Nations food agency pointed to the deal and to new harvests becoming available in the Northern Hemisphere. Buyers could see more grain coming onto the market before that first ship had even left.

That same July, the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics recorded an increase in bread prices. Say a bakery ordered the flour for today's bread weeks ago. If the price of wheat falls this morning, the bakery still owes what it agreed to pay for the flour. It has to keep the ovens running either way.

On the bakery's next order, flour might be cheaper. But if it's paying more to get the bread to the store, part of that saving could disappear on the truck. The retailer has bills of its own. By the time you pick up the loaf, the price on the shelf reflects decisions made at each step, sometimes weeks apart.

<!-- /NARRATION -->

## 5. Who can absorb the cost?

**Visual.** Set the fuel receipt beside the shoes the driver will wear for another month. Switch to a sourced map for Somalia, with separate indicators for poor harvests and costly imports. (F06–F07, A01)

<!-- NARRATION -->

If your shift starts before the first bus, there's only so much you can do about how often you fill up. You can put less gas in the tank today. That just means you'll be back at the station sooner. When a few dollars more for gas comes out of a paycheck that's already spoken for, it can mean putting off something else you need. Maybe the shoes you were going to replace have to last another month.

In Somalia that year, drought had damaged local harvests. It also meant neighboring countries had less food to sell. Somalia relied heavily on imported grain, including wheat from Russia and Ukraine. The World Food Programme and the United Nations food and agriculture agency reported that rising global food and fuel prices piled onto those local problems. Some staple foods reached prices that many poor households couldn't afford.

When the local harvest is poor, imported grain can help make up the difference. In Somalia that year, it was getting more expensive right when people needed it most.

<!-- /NARRATION -->

## 6. The bill that depends on interest rates

**Visual.** Put the Fed’s January signal, February invasion and March decision on a single timeline. Move to two labeled, illustrative contracts and isolate mortgage principal and interest and the card’s variable rate. (F08–F09, A01)

<!-- NARRATION -->

Then there was a bill that could get more expensive even if you bought nothing new. If you were carrying a credit card balance, you might open your statement one month and find a higher interest charge. It would be easy to put that increase in the same category as the gas bill. The dates tell a different story.

In January, before the full-scale invasion, U.S. consumer prices were already up seven and a half percent from a year earlier. The Federal Reserve had signaled that it expected to raise rates soon. When it did raise them in March, it pointed to price pressure stemming from the pandemic and described the invasion as likely to add to inflation in the near term. The war added pressure to a problem the Fed was already preparing to act on.

What can higher rates do about prices? If financing the next car gets more expensive, some buyers delay replacing the one they have. If fewer people buy, sellers face less pressure from demand. That's where the Fed has leverage. Interest rates have much less to say about whether a ship can load grain at a Ukrainian port.

And which borrowing bill changes depends on what you signed. A homeowner's principal and interest on an existing fixed-rate mortgage are set by the loan. A credit card rate tied to a benchmark can go up when that benchmark rises, so a balance carried over from last month can cost more this month.

<!-- /NARRATION -->

## 7. Back to the pump

**Visual.** Return to the opening pump at year-end. Bring the grocery bag into frame and end on the two recurring purchases, with the pump and grocery series clearly labeled. (F01, F06, A01)

<!-- NARRATION -->

By the end of twenty twenty-two, U.S. regular gasoline averaged three dollars and nine cents a gallon, below where it had started the year. Refineries were making more gasoline in the second half of the year, and Americans were using less. At the grocery store, food prices were still higher than they had been a year earlier.

The station could buy its next delivery for less as gasoline became easier to come by. For the bakery, cheaper wheat helped only if it brought down the price of flour. The cost of getting the next batch of bread to the store could still be high.

On that same drive home, you could spend less than you'd set aside for gas. A few minutes later, a week's groceries could eat up the difference.
