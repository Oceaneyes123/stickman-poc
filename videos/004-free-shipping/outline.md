## Teaching depth

| Concept | Already understood | Single new idea | Minimum explanation | Safely omit | Necessary qualification | Human review prompt |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Free price versus total price | Delivery can cost money. | Eliminating one fee does not determine the total outlay. | An imaginary cart changes after an extra item; two delivered totals. | Price formulas, annual extrapolations and unrelated spending statistics. | The added item may be worth the extra outlay; taxes and other fees are omitted from the example. | Can an offer remove a real fee while the customer still spends more? Explain without figures. |
| Conditional threshold | An offer has an eligibility rule. | The rule can create a reason to keep searching after the original need is met. | Show the original objective, the condition and the new search, then distinguish evidence from interpretation. | A glossary of behavioral biases or claims about subconscious control. | Framing and distance interact. Search does not guarantee purchase. A higher threshold can backfire. | What changed in the shopper's task after the offer appeared? |
| Retailer economics | Someone supplies delivery. | Higher sales and higher profit are different outcomes. | Shipping costs remain; additional merchandise may help cover them. | Margin calculations, retailer-specific financials and optimization advice. | Merchant guidance is not independent causal evidence, and promotions need not be profitable. | Why might the retailer offer the deal and still fail to profit? |
| Usefulness and timing | A household item can be needed later. | A larger cart alone does not establish waste or long-run overspending. | Explicitly change the add-on's usefulness; compare the full choices and real constraints. | A generalized household budget or advice to always stock up, wait or shop in person. | Cash, storage, urgency and access vary. | What assumption would make the added product worthwhile rather than an unwanted purchase? |
