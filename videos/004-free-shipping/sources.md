# Sources and assumptions

Research checked 2026-11-17, GMT+8. Primary research and first-party merchant guidance only. Findings are attributed to their actual setting. A report verified from an abstract is not described as a full-text audit.

## Source register

### S01. Zero-price experiments

Kristina Shampanier, Nina Mazar and Dan Ariely. *Zero as a Special Price: The True Value of Free Products*. Marketing Science 26, issue 6, pages 742-757. November-December 2007. DOI `10.1287/mksc.1060.0254`.

Source URL: `https://people.duke.edu/~dandan/webfiles/PapersPI/Zero%20as%20a%20Special%20Price.pdf`

Original paper hosted on coauthor Dan Ariely's Duke University site, downloaded successfully from this canonical URL. Relevant paper text and experiment 2's methods/results consulted, pages 746-747. An MIT student-center setting includes real chocolate purchases. Lowering both prices by the same small amount, with one reaching zero, shifted choice toward the free option. No experiment here establishes the effect of a modern shipping progress bar or a universal effect size. The narration explicitly separates these experiments from shipping evidence.

### S02. Shipping thresholds and basket formation

Chaoqun Chen and Donald Ngwe. *Shipping Fees and Product Assortment in Online Retail*. Harvard Business School working paper 19-034. September 21, 2018.

Source URL: `https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/19-034_b2382177-a462-447e-86f8-690d1ea7af18.pdf`

Full working-paper PDF downloaded successfully. Abstract, introduction, data description and discussion of figures 1-3 consulted. Philippine online fashion retailer, transactions from September 2012 to October 2016. Orders bunch above the threshold. Product composition is consistent with some customers topping up. The larger-basket comparison with unconditional free shipping is a model finding, not a randomized experiment. Neither bunching nor basket size establishes that added products were unwanted, regretted or financially harmful. Search-cost counterfactuals are not proof that a named retailer deliberately obstructs shoppers.

### S03. Merchant use and delivery economics

Shopify. *Free Shipping: 8 Strategies To Boost Sales in 2026*. Living merchant guide, updated September 26, 2026, according to its byline. Accessed November 17, 2026.

Source URL: `https://www.shopify.com/blog/free-shipping-and-conversion`

First-party merchant guidance. Sections on minimum order value, costs and communicating offers consulted. Discusses thresholds to increase cart value, shipping bars, and how delivery costs must be covered. This is evidence of the platform's advice and available practices, not independent experimental proof of effectiveness. No survey percentages, named merchant performance claims, threshold-setting formula or current named-store terms are imported into narration.

### S04. Sales are not profit

Michael Lewis, Vishal Singh and Scott Fay. *An Empirical Study of the Impact of Nonlinear Shipping and Handling Fees on Purchase Incidence and Expenditure Decisions*. Marketing Science 25, issue 1, pages 51-64. Published online January 1, 2006. DOI `10.1287/mksc.1050.0150`.

Source URL: `https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mksc.1050.0150`

Publisher abstract read through the search-indexed publisher page and independently retrieved publisher-deposited Crossref metadata, not full text. Direct publisher retrieval returned HTTP 403. Crossref metadata URL: `https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1287/mksc.1050.0150`. The authors report an online retailer's varied shipping-fee schedules and a model of ordering and basket size. Free-shipping promotions generated additional sales but could be unprofitable in that setting because of lost shipping revenue and heterogeneous response. The script reports this limited finding. It does not claim every retailer loses money on free shipping or give an effect size.

### S05. Distance to a promotional threshold and search

Atul A. Kulkarni and Hong Yuan. *Shopper Search in Response to Conditional Promotions: A Function of Basket Sizes and Incentive Types*. Psychology & Marketing 42, pages 1700-1720. First published February 15, 2025. DOI `10.1002/mar.22200`.

Source URL: `https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/mar.22200`

Publisher abstract read through the search-indexed publisher page and directly retrieved publisher-deposited Crossref metadata. Direct full-text retrieval returned HTTP 403; full methods were not inspected. Crossref metadata URL: `https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1002/mar.22200`. The abstract reports five studies using behavioral and self-reported search measures. Basket-to-threshold distance interacts with incentive framing. Script support is limited to the general finding that the gap and wording can influence continued search. It does not claim proximity increases search under every framing, that a specific progress bar was tested, or that search necessarily ends in a purchase. The study's participant jurisdictions were not established from the abstract.

### S06. A higher threshold can backfire

Quan Zhou, Qianran Jenny Jin, Mehmet Gumus and Animesh Animesh. *How to (and How Not to) Increase Free-Shipping Thresholds: Evidence from Online Retail*. Working paper dated February 24, 2026, posted on SSRN March 13, 2026, last revised October 15, 2026, according to the indexed SSRN record. DOI `10.2139/ssrn.6297139`.

Source URLs:

- `https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6297139`
- `https://www.bschool.cuhk.edu.hk/events/how-to-and-how-not-to-increase-free-shipping-thresholds-evidence-from-online-retail/`
- `https://qzhou-2020.github.io/publications/`

Accessible evidence: search-indexed author abstract, CUHK Business School's directly retrieved seminar abstract for May 15, 2026, and the first author's directly retrieved research page, which lists the paper as under major revision at MSOM. Direct SSRN page retrieval returned HTTP 403 and full paper text was not reviewed. These are presentations of the same study, not independent replications. The revised author abstract reports transaction/clickstream data from one North American online grocery platform and a difference-in-differences design. It specifies that the spending decline concerns continuing purchasers; that restriction is retained in narration. Script attribution says working paper and reports the direction only. No numerical estimate is narrated. This is a threshold increase, not an experiment proving all threshold offers reduce spending. Country, study dates and full robustness checks were not established from the accessible summaries.

## Assumptions

### A01. One illustrative online order

- Currency and period: USD, one order, not monthly income or a budget.
- Original merchandise subtotal: $42 for the required charger purchase, treated as one cart amount.
- Original delivery charge: $6.
- Free-shipping eligibility: merchandise subtotal of at least $50.
- Optional cable organizer: $12.
- Same retailer and delivery service in both choices. All modeled products qualify for the offer.
- Tax and other fees are omitted, set to zero solely to isolate the mechanism. No membership, discounts, returns, interest, debt or special exclusions are modeled. Actual checkout terms can differ.
- Original delivered order: $48. Enlarged delivered order: $54. Extra outlay: $6.
- The script narrates the two delivered totals, not a worked calculation. The added object has value; an extra outlay is not automatically a loss or a mistake.

Arithmetic verification uses `freeShippingExample`, `calculateFreeShippingOrder`, `sum`, `remaining` and `usd` from `../../src/finance/math.ts`. A Node command with strict assertions returns original $48, enlarged $54, increase $6, gap $8, overshoot $4, and eligibility true. Production graphics, if separately commissioned, must derive their states from the same assumptions through that module.

Re-run from the repository root:

```bash
node --experimental-strip-types --input-type=module -e 'import assert from "node:assert/strict"; import {calculateFreeShippingOrder} from "./src/finance/math.ts"; const order=calculateFreeShippingOrder(); assert.equal(order.before,48); assert.equal(order.after,54); assert.equal(order.increase,6); assert.ok(order.qualifies); console.log({before:order.before,after:order.after,increase:order.increase});'
```

### A02. Useful purchases and real constraints

The toothpaste, replacement household purchase, shift work, mobility limitations, storage, available cash and optional enjoyment of an organizer are explicitly hypothetical possibilities. They establish why an add-on can have value and why alternatives have costs, not a claim about their prevalence. Buying a needed product earlier can change timing rather than lifetime use. No household savings forecast or preferred shopping choice is asserted.

## Not claimed anywhere

- Every customer spends more, every threshold is deceptive, or all free shipping is a scam.
- A fixed overspending percentage, average annual loss, demographic diagnosis, dopamine release or brain mechanism.
- That loss aversion, mental accounting or a goal-gradient effect was directly measured in the cited fashion-retailer transactions.
- That the added products in transaction data were unnecessary, or that a higher basket means financial harm.
- That a paid delivery option is always cheaper, or that a trip to a physical shop is always accessible or cheaper.
- That all fulfillment costs are passed through to an individual customer, or that a larger sale is automatically profit.
- Real current retailer thresholds, a particular seller's intentions, membership terms, returns policies or customer testimony.
- Peer-reviewed status for the 2018 or 2026 working papers, full-text access for S04-S06, measured narration, audience comprehension, media clearance or production approval.
